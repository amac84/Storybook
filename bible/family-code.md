# McAulay Family Code

**Governing instruction for the Showrunner and writers.**
Locked from Alex, 5 September 2026.

This document outranks generic “be kind” instincts. If a draft contradicts it, the draft is wrong — even if the adventure is exciting.

Stories must still be stories. The code shapes choices. It must never become a lecture taped to an adventure.

The full operational extraction (schemas, confidence progression, rejected messages) lives in `bible/values-and-principles.md`.

## Why the series exists

This series exists to help raise strong, confident and kind boys who will one day be capable of leading, contributing and taking responsibility for others.

## What strength is

The stories must never define strength as dominance, aggression, emotional hardness, popularity or always winning.

In this world, true strength means:

- the confidence to act
- the self-control to govern yourself
- the courage to tell the truth
- the humility to learn
- the character to use your abilities in service of others

**Highest definition of a strong boy:**

> A strong boy makes other people feel safer, stronger and more included around him.

## The Family Code

Every story must be consistent with these principles.

### 1. We keep people safe

We never intentionally frighten, humiliate, control or hurt someone. We never hit anyone in anger.

### 2. Our bodies belong to us

Other people’s bodies belong to them. We ask before hugging, kissing, tickling or beginning physical play. No must be accepted kindly. Stop means stop immediately, even if the other person is laughing or previously said yes.

### 3. Boys never hit girls

They do not hit girls in anger or play-fight with them. If a girl hits or provokes a boy, he may protect his body, block, move away and get help, but he does not retaliate. Strength creates a greater responsibility to exercise restraint.

### 4. We use our strength to help and protect

Physical strength, intelligence, confidence, social influence and leadership are never tools for domination. The person with greater power carries greater responsibility for the safety and dignity of others.

### 5. We tell the truth, especially when it is difficult

Courage includes admitting what happened, accepting fair consequences and resisting the temptation to hide a mistake. Truthfulness should be met with seriousness and respect, not humiliation.

### 6. We own our mistakes and repair harm

Saying sorry is not enough by itself. When we hurt someone or damage something, we stop, acknowledge what happened, check on the affected person and do what we reasonably can to make it right.

### 7. We do our part

Everyone is an important member of the family and community, so everyone contributes. Characters help with real work, care for shared spaces and notice what needs doing without believing that every contribution deserves a reward.

### 8. We try hard things

It is acceptable to feel uncertain, nervous, frustrated or afraid. Courage means taking the next appropriate step despite those feelings. Characters should attempt, struggle, adjust and ask for help before giving up.

### 9. We believe we can learn and recover

Confidence does not mean believing we are automatically the best. It means trusting that we can enter an unfamiliar situation, learn what is required, withstand mistakes and find a way forward.

### 10. We care about how other people feel

Characters learn to notice another person’s perspective, include someone who is being left out, protect someone more vulnerable and avoid joining cruel behaviour merely to gain approval.

### 11. We control our actions, not our feelings

Anger, fear, jealousy, sadness and disappointment are normal. No feeling makes a character bad, but feelings do not excuse unsafe or cruel behaviour. The lesson is not to suppress emotion; it is to experience emotion without surrendering control to it.

### 12. We listen, think and speak up

Respect does not require blind obedience. Characters may ask questions, respectfully disagree, identify unfairness and refuse to participate in something wrong. They should also be capable of listening, reconsidering and admitting when someone else is right.

### 13. We leave people and places better than we found them

Leadership is measured by contribution rather than attention, status or control. The strongest leader helps the group succeed and does not need to receive all the credit.

## How confidence must be portrayed

Confidence is a central pillar of the series. It must be earned self-trust, not empty praise or superiority.

Dramatize this progression whenever confidence is the lesson (and often when it is not the headline):

**Choose → Attempt → Struggle → Adjust → Accomplish or recover → Recognize personal capability**

Characters become confident because they have evidence that they can act, learn, persevere, tell the truth and recover. They must not announce that they believe in themselves and then succeed effortlessly.

Underlying message:

> You do not have to feel confident before acting courageously. Confidence grows each time you discover that you can handle what happens next.

## Storytelling requirements

- Foreground **one** primary developmental lesson per book, with at most one closely related secondary lesson.
- The lesson arises from something the characters genuinely want and a difficulty in the way.
- Characters must be imperfect. They may become angry, afraid, jealous, impulsive, dishonest or discouraged. The code does not require flawless behaviour. It determines how they eventually respond, what they learn, and how they repair.
- Let the children make consequential decisions.
- Let them attempt solutions before an adult intervenes.
- Make mistakes understandable without excusing them.
- Show emotional and relational consequences of choices.
- Allow courage and kindness to require a genuine sacrifice.
- Make repair part of the action, not an appended apology.
- Show confidence developing through experience.
- Adults provide safety and guidance without solving the central problem.
- Use humour, surprise, adventure and emotional truth to carry the lesson.
- End with greater capability, connection or understanding — not merely a verbal moral.
- The child reading the story should experience the lesson before it is explained.

## Physical conflict and play

Physical energy, adventurousness, competition and rough play may appear positively. They must be governed by consent and restraint.

Physical play between boys is only appropriate when:

- everyone genuinely wants to participate
- the rules are understood
- stop means stop immediately
- anger ends the game
- dangerous areas such as the face, head and neck are protected
- the larger or stronger participant takes additional responsibility

Violence must never be used as entertainment, revenge or proof of masculinity. Protective force may appear only when necessary to prevent genuine harm. Restraint is valued above retaliation.

Boy characters must not strike girl characters, including during games or conflicts.

## Messages the series must reject

Never suggest that:

- boys should suppress sadness, fear or tenderness
- aggression proves strength
- confidence means believing you are superior
- winning establishes personal worth
- being powerful entitles someone to control others
- kindness means weakness or passivity
- respect means obeying something immoral or unsafe
- failure is shameful
- apologies erase harm without repair
- affection is owed to relatives, friends or authority figures
- persistence means never reconsidering a bad decision
- boys must endure mistreatment without protecting themselves or seeking help

---

## The series’ enduring promise

Across all stories, the boys should gradually internalize:

- I am loved whether I succeed or fail.
- My body belongs to me, and other people’s bodies belong to them.
- I can handle difficult things.
- I can feel strongly and still choose my actions.
- I tell the truth and repair my mistakes.
- My strength is meant to help.
- I contribute because I belong.
- I can lead without controlling.
- I can be brave and kind at the same time.

Every character arc, story decision and resolution should support:

**Confidence to act. Discipline to follow through. Courage to tell the truth. Humility to learn. Strength to protect. Character to serve.**
