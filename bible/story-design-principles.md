# McAulay Story Design Principles

**Craft law for the Showrunner, Story Architect, Editorial Critic, and Author.**
Locked from Alex, 5 September 2026.

The Family Code (`bible/family-code.md`) says **what** a strong boy is.
These principles say **how** a story plants that without feeling like school.

If the two conflict in tone, the story wins the page and the code still wins the character’s choice. Never sacrifice either by explaining the code out loud.

---

## Purpose

These stories should do more than entertain.

Each story should help a child build a useful inner capability: confidence, resilience, patience, emotional regulation, empathy, courage, independence, curiosity, responsibility, or another trait that will help them become capable and contributing adults.

The lesson should be real.

The story should never feel like a lesson.

The child should experience a genuinely good story first and absorb the developmental idea almost invisibly underneath it.

The ultimate test is not whether the child can explain the moral.

The ultimate test is whether, days or weeks later, the child encounters a similar situation in real life and has a better idea of what to do.

---

## 1. Start with a precise developmental objective

Every story teaches **one** primary idea.

Avoid broad objectives: *Be confident. Be kind. Be resilient. Control your emotions.*

Identify a specific situation and a useful response.

| Broad | Specific |
| --- | --- |
| Confidence | I am unsure whether I will be good at something new. |
| Belief to develop | I do not need to know that I will succeed before I begin. |
| Useful behaviour | Take one small first step. |

Required template (every outline):

```yaml
situation:   # What happens to the child?
feeling:     # What is the child likely to feel?
belief:      # What do we want the child eventually to understand?
strategy:    # What can the child actually do?
```

The strategy matters. The child should leave knowing what to **do**, not only what to think.

If Alex’s brief is still broad (“confidence”), the Showrunner and Architect **sharpen it** before prose. Do not ask Alex to write the four fields unless the sharpening would lock a new series value.

---

## 2. Story first, curriculum second

The objective guides the story invisibly. It must not dominate.

Do not begin from “Cove needs to learn perseverance.”

Begin from something they want: *Cove has promised Eden that tonight they will sleep in the greatest blanket fort ever built.* Then the fort falls down.

If the lesson disappeared and the story would no longer be interesting, the story is not ready.

The reader should want to know: **What happens next?**

---

## 3. Give the child a tool they can carry away

Every story contains **one** short portable sentence.

It must be short, concrete, easy to remember, natural to say, useful in real life.

It must sound like something a child might eventually say — not corporate motivation, not an adult lecture.

Examples of the *shape* (do not reuse these as a series slogan unless earned):

- “Not sure? Try one little step.”
- “Slow down. Then try again.”
- “Being brave can still feel scary.”

Success: months later a parent can cue it — “Remember — not sure?” — and the child answers with the rest.

Invent a **new** phrase when the objective is new. Recurring phrases may return if they are already beloved canon.

---

## 4. Repeat the idea without repeating the story

Progression, not explanation:

1. **See it** — someone else models the idea.
2. **Try it** — the main character tries it with help.
3. **Own it** — the character recognizes the situation and uses it independently.

See it → try it → own it.

---

## 5. Let the child discover things

Do not explain what the reader can figure out.

Give the reader enough to get slightly ahead of the character. Then turn the page.

Whenever possible: **ask → allow thinking → reveal.**

---

## 6. Make the child feel capable

Design “I knew that!” moments.

Sometimes the character should not immediately know. Let the reader see it first. The child should occasionally feel the character needs his help.

Do not tell children they are capable. Arrange for them to *feel* capable.

---

## 7. Use progressive challenge

The first application is usually easier than the last. Same skill, rising difficulty.

Do not demonstrate the identical behaviour three times at the same stakes.

---

## 8. Include a setback

A useful strategy should not work perfectly every time.

Characters hesitate, forget, err, get frustrated, try the wrong thing, need help.

Teach: “I tried the right thing and it was still difficult.”

Not: “I did the right thing and everything immediately worked.”

---

## 9. Keep the emotional stakes child-sized

Treat as genuinely large: a taken toy, a lost object, a collapsed build, wanting a parent, somewhere unfamiliar, being left out, time to leave, losing a race, breaking something, waiting, embarrassment, not knowing how, a sibling changing the game.

Do not make the child ridiculous for caring. The feeling is true from his height.

Adventure may be huge. The *psychology* stays ordinary.

---

## 10. Use adventure to carry ordinary psychology

The world may be magical. Underneath: asking for help, trying again, sharing attention, fear, truth, waiting, calming down, including someone, disappointment.

Fantasy makes it exciting. Recognition makes it transferable.

---

## 11. Familiar story rhythm

Not a clone every book. Children still benefit from a recognizable kind of story.

Useful underlying architecture:

1. Desire
2. Problem
3. First attempt (instinctive response does not fully work)
4. Discovery
5. Practice (new strategy)
6. Setback (harder version)
7. Independent use (without being told)
8. Payoff
9. Small callback (the idea once more, naturally)

The machinery must be invisible to the child.

---

## 12. Do not over-explain the ending

Never: “And Cove learned that perseverance is very important.”

Demonstrate the change. Let the child complete the meaning.

---

## 13. Transfer the lesson once

Near the end, apply the same idea to a *slightly different* problem.

Do not repeat the original scenario. Do not have the hero do the second task for the other child. Prompt or model the strategy; let the new situation prove the principle.

---

## 14. Build for rereading

First read: the adventure. Later reads: visual clues, foreshadowing, background stories, recurring characters, later-important objects, two-level jokes, callbacks.

Knowing what comes next can be mastery, not boredom.

---

## 15. Give parents something too

Humour, emotional truth, subtle parenting observation, beautiful language, occasional two-level jokes, moments that affect an adult.

Never undermine the children’s story to entertain adults.

---

## 16. Keep parents competent but human

Parents may be tired, misunderstand, laugh, err, apologize, change their minds, get frustrated.

Do not make them foolish so children look clever.

They model calm, curiosity, accountability, affection, boundaries, humour, and admitting mistakes.

Sometimes the lesson observed is: Dad got that wrong, acknowledged it, and repaired it.

They still do not solve the central problem.

---

## 17. Use questions sparingly and deliberately

Interactive questions are powerful and become annoying if every page asks one.

Use them only where thinking improves the story. Then give time — often a page turn — before the answer.

Participation in the adventure, not a comprehension test.

---

## 18. Test stories on real children

After a book exists, observe (Alex, or notes he brings back):

Attention, understanding, anticipation, participation, emotion, recall, which phrases they repeat, whether the idea transfers.

Do not automatically simplify everything they miss. Ask why first.

Record useful observations in `bible/engagement-principles.md` and generalizable taste in `bible/creator-taste.md`.

---

## 19. Test the lesson outside the story

Do not only ask “What was the lesson?”

Present another situation later, or wait for real life.

If the child independently retrieves the strategy or phrase, the lesson transferred.

That is the real measure. The studio cannot complete this test alone; the final report should suggest one outside-the-book probe for Alex.

---

## 20. Protect the magic

These principles are writer tools. They should be almost invisible.

A child should not think: “I have completed a social-emotional learning exercise.”

He should think: **“Read it again.”**

---

## Standard for every story

Before lock, the Showrunner and Critic answer:

| Test | Question |
| --- | --- |
| Story | Would this still be a good story if nobody told me there was a lesson? |
| Truth | Does the emotional problem feel real to a child? |
| Objective | Can I describe the developmental objective in one precise sentence? |
| Strategy | Does the child leave with something he can actually do? |
| Memory | Is there one phrase or idea likely to stick? |
| Participation | Does the reader ever get to think rather than simply listen? |
| Competence | Does the child get a chance to feel clever or capable? |
| Progression | Does the challenge become more difficult? |
| Failure | Does the character struggle before succeeding? |
| Transfer | Does the principle work in more than one situation? |
| Restraint | Have I avoided explaining what the story already demonstrates? |
| Rereading | Will there be something satisfying on the tenth reading? |
| Parent | Would I enjoy reading this aloud repeatedly? |
| Real life | Could this plausibly change what a child does later? |

If most answers are yes, the story is probably doing what it should.

Do not mechanically tick every box at the cost of wonder. A missing transfer beat with a documented reason is better than a bolted-on second lecture.

---

## Core philosophy

Tell a wonderful story.

Underneath it, give the child:

- a problem he recognizes
- a feeling he understands
- a challenge he can think about
- a chance to feel capable
- a useful strategy
- a phrase he can carry into the world

Do not simply tell children who they should become.

Give them repeated opportunities to experience themselves becoming that person.
